The Idea in Between

When I first listened to Glenn Gould’s *The Idea of North*, I was
floored. I knew him as a virtuoso pianist, but I had not fully grasped
the depth of his engagement with experimental media. The piece arranges
and composes interviews alongside sound. Voices enter clearly, then
begin to overlap, interrupt, and blur. At moments the listening becomes
difficult. You cannot hold or discern every word. And yet that
difficulty is precisely what opens space for thought. The work invites
listening and resists it at the same time. It creates a sonic
environment where ideas emerge through friction rather than explanation.
Gould’s decision to move away from live performance and into the studio
allows for this exploration. It signals a shift from spectacle to
construction, from audience immediacy to a mediated intimacy. As someone
who has played music in front of live audiences and as someone who can’t
imagine not seeing live shows Gould’s legacy is all the more powerful to
think through and engage with.

This dialogic project takes that shift seriously. Right now, it exists
as a text narrative housed on a website. It is not yet an audio piece.
It is a script, a score, an outline of a possible audio work. The
website format matters because it helps to provide structure. The main
text is centered on the site with core texts surrounding it. The text
itself tries to provide spatial cues for where the voices exist and what
possible sonic soundscapes might emerge. The text anticipates overlap
and simultaneity even though it is silent. White space functions like
pause. In this sense, the website becomes a conceptual studio. It is a
space where I can experiment with relation before moving into audio
production.

The theories I am engaging are not secondary to the work. They shape its
form. The conversation between Martin and Buber is one that I have had
for years. Agnes Martin in my mind is one of the great pure artists. Her
mysticism and solitary existence is what makes her work so special. Her
claims about inspiration and ethics seem to be in opposition to Buber’s
writing. I encountered Martin Buber at almost the same time as I first
learned about the work of Agnes Martin. His claim that “all real living
is meeting” pushes me to think about Martin’s life and existence. Her
living outside of meeting. How then can a media project begin to explore
an encounter between these two individuals. To further explore the ideas
of interiority and exteriority I thought an additional dialouge between
more contemporary artists/thinkers would be interesting, especially ones
that explored video work. Bringing together Derek Jarman and Trinh T.
Minh-Ha as a second conversation that explores the solitary but also
solidarity seemed like an interesting transition to the present moment.

The website was built using Codespaces in Github. Through numerous
prompts I asked Codespaces to develop a minimal and simple website with
the texts provided.

Setting: The sun is rising across the desert landscape in Taos, NM. The
time does not matter. A lone figure, Martin Buber, slowly approaches a
small ranch home. On the porch of the home sits a women in a chair. The
air is clear and the only sounds you can hear are the foosteps of Buber
approaching the home and the chair creaking in which the women sits.

There is a long silent moment once Buber stops about 10 feet from the
porch. He is facing Martin but her chair is at at slight angle so it
prevents direct eye contact.

This scene feels like it can go on forever but there is a dramatic cut
to flowers growing wildly in a interior courtyard. Derek Jarman and
Trinh T. Minh-Ha sit together on a swinging bench inches from the
flowers. The clouds above them move furiously across the sky blocking
and making openings for the sun.

The dialogue begins with a lone voice but throughout the soundscape
weaves in and out from one voice to all speakers alongside additional
ambiet noises and soundscapes.

Below are the individual texts for each speaker.

Agnes Martin: The desert air is life. It lifts the sun and lets the
colors shine brightly. Some think that I came out to the desert to find
refuge and escape from New York City, but in fact, the desert called for
me. It saw me for me. The quiet and open spaces filled me with a joy,
caused vibrations that would find their way through my brushes and onto
the canvas.

*All people have access to two parts of their mind*. The world appears
before us as a series of transactions, of facts, of determinations, of
the day to day that brings us rest, exhaustion, solitude, friendship,
hunger, and all of the multiplicities of the physical. Our outerminds
record and help us get through this landscape of existence. The inner
mind is there as well. Waiting for you to make a decision about “doing.”
It is in this doing in which we can find joy, elation, or inspiration.
*Inspiration exists in the mind.* It is not in the world of objects.

Inspiration creates us. It reinvents. It pushes us into the mystery that
is life.

People speak of the desert as empty. It is not empty. It is exact. It
does not interfere. It does not impose narrative. It allows you to see
without desire. Beauty is not in the object seen. “Beauty is the mystery
of life.” It is not in the eye, it is in the mind. It is not something
you possess. It happens when you are absent enough to receive it.

I paint lines because they are not things. A line does not describe. It
does not dominate. It does not wound. It allows the mind to rest in
perfection. And “happiness is being on the beam with life.” It is a
simple alignment — no drama, no struggle.

You stand there, Martin, speaking of twofold worlds. I do not divide the
world. I divide attention. When attention is given to inspiration, the
outer falls away. There is no conflict in the desert. Only clarity.

The painting is not about the desert. It is about innocence. It is about
the moment when thought ceases to interfere. When you see without the
hunger to own what you see.

If I sit in silence, it is because silence allows the inner mind to
rise. Silence is not absence. It is fullness without demand.

Martin Buber: Twofold. The world exists twofold. Our attitudes exist
twofold. It takes two.

When we experience life and to understand it we do so through two
relationships: I–It and I–Thou.

Agnes do you sit with It or with Thou? Do you see a difference?

You to speak of the inner and the outer mind. I speak of relation. The I
that says “It” is not the same I that says “Thou.” When I say “It,” I
experience, I measure, I use. The world becomes an object among objects.
Necessary, yes — but partial.

But when I say Thou, I do not experience something wholly inside me.
That I is only possible because of Thou.

*All real living is meeting*

This meeting is between two people.

The desert may appear empty. But emptiness does not guarantee relation.
One can stand before the horizon and still remain in I–It, analyzing
color, light, temperature. The Thou is not found by abstraction, nor by
withdrawal alone. It is found in presence.

You say beauty is in the mind. I would say: relation is not in the mind
alone. It happens between.

The Thou cannot be grasped. The moment one tries to fix it, it becomes
It. Even God, whom I have called the Eternal Thou, cannot be possessed.
Only addressed.

The danger of perfection is that it tempts us toward solitude without
response. But “in the beginning is relation.” Not isolation. Not
transcendence beyond encounter.

When you paint, do you address? Or do you dissolve the other? Can a line
be spoken as Thou? Or does it hover beyond relation?

Yet I sense that your silence is not indifference. There is a quality of
presence here. The desert does not answer, yet it receives. Perhaps
relation does not always require speec, does not require the physical
presence of an other. But it requires recoginzing a depth within, a
willingness to say yes to this unknown and experience this inspiration
on its own terms.

Yet, I can’t help but think that without the Thou, the I itself becomes
function. It cannot become inspiration.

Simulataneoulsy Derek Jarman and Trinh t. Minh-Ha sit in an interior
garden courtyard.

Derek Jarman:

I planted a garden when I saw that my end was coming. In Dungeness,
where the wind tore everything sideways. The garden was not a retreat;
it was a life. It became my life.

Even as my visions turned blue from the medications that could never
save me I found my inspiration by the sea.

*Blue is the universal love in which man bathes - it is the terrestial
paradise*

When sight began to dissolve, when the retina faltered, when AIDS
hollowed the body, my body, blue remained. In fact, it was given to me.
My favorite color letting me see.

The desert is a clarity. My landscape was dying, just like me, salt air,
illness. Yet even there, the light insisted to show itself brightly and
for me.

The body was made to betray, to die one day almost never by our own
choosing. The eye clouds, it is a cloud, obscuring and revealing all
that we might see. But color persists beyond the body’s certainty.
Beyond what we can see.

Silence is not innocent. It echos the voices within the hospital
corridors, the humming of machines that keep us alive. Blue was and is
awas space between life and disappearance. To grasp it is to let it go
instantly.

In the courtyard here, I imagine plants pressing upward despite the heat
from the sun. They go towards the light even if it will burn and dry
them up.

“In time no one will remember our work.” That is what illness teaches.
Ephemerality. But in the present tense of seeing, something holds.

Is blue a Thou? Or is it the last It before darkness?

I do not seek perfection. I seek to share myself. A mark against
erasure. Even if the mark dissolves.

Trinh T. Minh-Ha:

*To speak nearby is not to speak about* It is not to seize the object
and fix it in language. It is to maintain a relation that does not
colonize.

When you describe the desert, Agnes, I listen for the risk of purity.
Landscape has often been made innocent by those who can afford
innocence. Representation is never neutral.

The gaze frames. The frame excludes. Every articulation produces silence
elsewhere.

Martin you speak of I–Thou. Yet even the Thou may conceal asymmetry. Who
has the authority to address? Who is granted the status of Thou? Maybe
you knew this?

In documentary practice, I resist the voice of mastery. I prefer
intervals, gaps, hesitations. The unsaid is not absence but strategy. It
can say more and more perfectly.

*To speak nearby, is a great challenge . . . It is an attitude in life,
a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world.* It is proximity
without totality.

Derek speaks of blue as immersion. But can immersion erase difference?
The task is to remain aware of positionality even within beauty.

Listening is never passive. Listening is labor. Listening is love.

Perhaps the porch and the courtyard are not separate spaces but
overlapping acoustics. Perhaps the question is not whether the desert is
Thou, but how we situate ourselves within what we call desert.

The ethical demand is not silence, nor speech, but attentiveness to the
space between.
